# How the demo holds attention (v3)

The v3 demo is a story, not a tour of the site. Each choice below is tied to evidence, with how strong that
evidence is. Pop-psychology claims are left out or flagged.

| Principle | Evidence | In the video |
|---|---|---|
| **Earn the first seconds.** Viewers leave during long set-ups. | Moderate (industry data): Wistia found that starting content within 10 seconds halved early drop-off; YouTube treats the first 30 s as its own retention metric. | Opens on the profit figure itself, out of silence, within the first second. No logo, no team intro. |
| **Curiosity gap.** A noticed gap in knowledge creates a pull to close it, and improves memory for what follows. | Moderate–strong: Loewenstein's information-gap theory; Gruber et al. 2014 (*Neuron*), Kang et al. 2009. | "So why aren't we trading it?" is asked at 0:04 and answered at the twist (about 1:05). |
| **Prediction before a reveal.** Guessing first improves learning, most when the guess is wrong. | Moderate: Brod 2021 (prediction as a learning strategy); generation effect, Bertsch et al. 2007 (d ≈ 0.4). | "Your guess: is any gap worth trading today?" Three dots fill, the music thins, then "No." |
| **Change the picture every few seconds, with a reason.** Motion and cuts trigger the orienting response; related cuts help memory, unrelated ones hurt it. | Moderate (LC4MP); no study gives an exact cut length for demos. | A new zoom, slider, click or card every 2–4 s, each tied to the sentence on screen. |
| **Key words, not transcripts.** Short on-screen phrases beat full-text captions. | Strong for learning: Mayer's multimedia principles; Adesope & Nesbit 2012 (57 studies). | Every caption is one short sentence with the number in gold. |
| **Sound in sync with a visual event makes the event stand out.** | Moderate–strong: Van der Burg et al. 2008 ("pip and pop"). The benefit disappears for unrelated sounds. | Effects only on meaningful events: the number landing, real clicks, the counter rolling, the reveal, the cost tone as profit melts. Nothing on cursor moves. |
| **One peak, and a strong ending.** Peak and end moments predict how an experience is remembered; duration barely matters. | Strong: Kahneman et al. 1993; meta-analysis Alaybek et al. 2022 (r ≈ 0.58). | Peak: "Too good to be true?" then the honest twist (January 2026 made the money). End: a true open question that only the live site answers. |
| **Two-sided messages persuade experts, if the objection is answered.** | Moderate (meta-analytic). Raising a doubt without answering it does worse. | The video raises "too good to be true?" and answers it with the crash split, the overfitting check and "we say so". |
| **Invite, don't command.** Forceful calls to action cause reactance. | Moderate: Ma, Ebesu & Dillard (meta-analysis of 35 studies). | "See today's answer:" plus the address and a QR code. No countdowns, no "you must". |
| **Leave a felt gap at the end.** The urge to resume something unfinished. | The Ovsiankina effect is supported; the Zeigarnik memory effect is not (Ghibellini & Meier 2025 meta-analysis), so we do not rely on it. | "Tomorrow's answer isn't in this video. This video stops at 6 Oct 2026. The site doesn't." True, because the site updates every trading day. |
| **Short beats long.** | Moderate: duration neglect (above); hackathon videos are typically 2–3 min. | 1:27 (v3.4). |

**Sound levels** (mixing practice, not research): music bed well under the captions' moments, effects
14–25 dB below the loudest moment, one loudest effect (the twist), near-silence while the viewer guesses,
a riser that cuts dead on the reveal, a low room tone so pauses don't sound like a fault, and a resolving
chord on the address. The final mix is normalised to −16 LUFS integrated, −1.5 dBTP true peak.

**v3.2, softer sound.** Viewers found the bed sharp. The hi-hats and claps (bright noise bursts) are gone, the
arpeggio and top line are an octave lower and pure tones, the music is rolled off above about 3 kHz and the
effects above 4.5 kHz, ticks and clicks are lower pitched, and the whole mix is 2 dB quieter. Energy above
5 kHz is now 42 dB below the total.

**Visual attention layer (v3.1).** Small, continuous, meaningful motion, so the eye has a reason to stay:
the camera "breathes" (v3.2: about 3% zoom and up to 10 px of drift on 8–13 s cycles), so the picture is never
frozen; edges darken slightly when the camera is close in on a number (a spotlight); a single warm light
sweep crosses each caption as it appears (v3.2: brighter and wider); a brief brightness bloom lands exactly on the four key sounds (the
number, the reveal, the crash, the twist), the same audio-visual synchrony as above; slow, soft gold dust drifts on
the statement cards (v3.2: about 130 fainter specks) and, sparser still, over the site. Each is kept below the level where it reads as an effect rather than as life in the
picture. Sound effects sit under the music bed (v3.1: 8–10 dB quieter than v3), with the twist still the
loudest single sound.

**Avoided:** logo or team intros before the hook; captions that repeat a narration word for word; a sound on
every click; fake urgency; inflated or unexplained numbers; "in conclusion"; credits after the call to action.

Sources: Wistia, "Earn the first 10 seconds" (engagement-graph learnings); YouTube Help, audience retention;
Loewenstein 1994 via PBS NOVA; Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, *Neuron*; Kang et al. 2009, *Psychological
Science*; Brod 2021, PMC8642250; Bertsch et al. 2007, *Memory & Cognition*; Lang, LC4MP; Mayer 2008;
Adesope & Nesbit 2012, *Journal of Educational Psychology*; Van der Burg et al. 2008, *JEP: HPP*;
Kahneman et al. 1993, *Psychological Science*; Alaybek et al. 2022, *OBHDP*; Ma, Ebesu & Dillard (reactance
meta-analysis); Ghibellini & Meier 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*; Guo, Kim & Rubin
2014 (edX video engagement).

**v3.4, a guide and highlights.** Pari, the gold-bar mascot, reacts to the story: short speech bubbles on the key
moments ("Whoa!" on the number, "Nope." on the answer, "Ouch!" on the crash, "Hmm... too good?" before the twist,
"Your guess?" while the viewer predicts), and a dotted gold pointer from Pari's hand to whatever is highlighted.
A guide character who looks and points at the content directs attention there (gaze and pointing cues in multimedia
learning: van Gog 2014; Fiorella & Mayer 2016), and the words are reactions only, never new claims. Each place the
camera moves to gets gold focus brackets that close in, pulse and fade, on top of the dimmed surround and the
traced outline. The cut is 87 seconds: shorter pauses between steps, every step kept.

**v3.5.** Pari no longer moves its mouth on every caption (with no voice, the flapping read as smiling and
unsmiling); its face changes only to react. Pari is larger, leans toward what it points at, and hops and points at
every place the camera goes. Highlights are thicker gold brackets and outlines with a soft glow, held longer, with
the surround dimmed more.

## v4 (88 s): rebuilt from a research review

Built after a review of the evidence on sound levels, harsh sounds, attention cues and on-screen characters
(summary below; evidence strength in brackets). Earlier lessons kept: no outlines or pointers on the page, no mouth
movement on captions, no footage sped up (v3.3–3.6 dropped every 5th frame to fit; v4 plays at real speed and
fits by a tighter storyboard and by trimming only still frames at the end of long reading holds).

**Sound.** Master −16 LUFS integrated, −1.5 dBTP true peak, small loudness range (AES TD1008 recommends
−16 to −18 LUFS for mixed streaming content; YouTube only turns louder content down) [standard]. Effects are one
family of soft mallet tones in the music's key, attacks 6–30 ms, base pitches 300–1,500 Hz, shelved −6 dB above 4 kHz
and rolled off above 9 kHz: the 2–5 kHz band is where hearing is most sensitive and where unpleasant sounds
concentrate (ISO 226; Kumar et al. 2012) [experiment]. Each effect is placed relative to the music at that moment
(+3 to +5 dB; +6/+7 for the two reveals; cuts and clicks just under the music), never two within 150 ms, and each
lands on one visual event with a brightness pulse of 2-frame onset and 0.4 s decay — sound helps attention only when
synchronised with an abrupt visual change ("pip and pop", Van der Burg et al. 2008) [experiments]. Decorative sounds
that carry no information cost comprehension (seductive details; Rey 2012, Sundararajan & Adesope 2020) [meta-analyses].
Music is instrumental, 96 bpm, warm (energy in 250–1,000 Hz, which phone speakers reproduce), no hi-hats or noise:
lyrics and fast, loud music hurt reading (Souza & Barbosa 2023; Thompson, Schellenberg & Letnic 2012) [experiments].
Progress is audible: the four "what we do differently" steps rise C–D–E–G; the brief checklist climbs a scale.
Each reveal is preceded by a riser that stops 0.45 s early, with the music ducked 12 dB in the gap (anticipation and
resolution, Huron 2006) [theory].

**Pictures.** The cue is framing plus a darkened surround (about 55% brightness, 0.4 s ease), no added shapes;
signalling improves retention (Schneider et al. 2018, g = 0.53) [meta-analysis]. Camera moves inside a scene take at
least 0.8 s, eased, then hold (Material motion durations scaled up for full-frame moves) [guideline].

**Pari.** On-screen agents give small benefits (Schroeder, Adesope & Gilbert 2013, g ≈ 0.19; text-speaking agents did
better than narrated ones) [meta-analysis]; pointing at the exact element and gaze cues help most (Kuang et al. 2023)
[meta-analysis]; emotion helps likability at true moments (Lawson et al. 2021) [experiment]. So Pari asks the
questions and says the asides in short bubbles (at most about 6 words; 1.5 s + 0.3 s a word; never at the same time
as a caption), points when the camera lands on the evidence, reacts only at real moments, blinks every 3–5 s, and
cross-fades between poses. It never interrupts a number landing (the lesson of Clippy: Swartz 2003).

**Story.** Hook with a true number and Pari's question (curiosity gap, Loewenstein 1994); a prediction before the
reveal; "What we do differently, 1 of 4" with a counter that fills (goal gradient, Kivetz et al. 2006); the honest
twist answered with evidence (refutational two-sided message, O'Keefe 1999); "How close are we?" ticks off the brief
line by line and names the one open gap (order-book depth); the ending is the open question only the live site
answers (peak-end, Kahneman et al. 1993).

**v4.1.** Smoother sound and a chattier guide, after a second review of how successful video makers hold attention
(editor and creator practice: Vox, Sound On Sound, Devpost's demo guidance; seductive-details meta-analyses for
restraint). Music changes snap to the beat (96 bpm, the grid placed so the twist lands on a downbeat); a layer
coming in rises over one bar while its filter opens, a layer going out falls over two beats (raised-cosine curves);
the "drop" keeps a soft pad, so the music never goes silent; risers run into the landing and end in a reverb tail;
the music dips only 3 dB before a reveal; each cut's sound starts 0.3 s before the picture (a J-cut). Pari says
twelve short lines, about one every 8 s, each reacting to what was just shown (never repeating a caption) and never
within 4 s of another. A title chip says what Parity is in the first second (Devpost: say what it does in the first
few seconds). The footage sits in a rounded frame with a soft shadow; focus is framing plus a softened, darkened
surround; camera moves carry motion blur (four samples over half a frame); cards settle in from 96%.
