# How the demo holds attention (v3)

The v3 demo is a story, not a tour of the site. Each choice below is tied to evidence, with how strong that
evidence is. Pop-psychology claims are left out or flagged.

| Principle | Evidence | In the video |
|---|---|---|
| **Earn the first seconds.** Viewers leave during long set-ups. | Moderate (industry data): Wistia found that starting content within 10 seconds halved early drop-off; YouTube treats the first 30 s as its own retention metric. | Opens on the profit figure itself, out of silence, within the first second. No logo, no team intro. |
| **Curiosity gap.** A noticed gap in knowledge creates a pull to close it, and improves memory for what follows. | Moderate–strong: Loewenstein's information-gap theory; Gruber et al. 2014 (*Neuron*), Kang et al. 2009. | "So why aren't we trading it?" is asked at 0:04 and answered at the twist (about 1:05). |
| **Prediction before a reveal.** Guessing first improves learning, most when the guess is wrong. | Moderate: Brod 2021 (prediction as a learning strategy); generation effect, Bertsch et al. 2007 (d ≈ 0.4). | "Your guess: is any gap worth trading today?" Three dots fill, the music thins, then "No." |
| **Change the picture every few seconds, with a reason.** Motion and cuts trigger the orienting response; related cuts help memory, unrelated ones hurt it. | Moderate (LC4MP); no study gives an exact cut length for demos. | A new zoom, slider, click or card every 2–4 s, each tied to the sentence on screen. |
| **Key words, not transcripts.** Short on-screen phrases beat full-text captions. | Strong for learning: Mayer's multimedia principles; Adesope & Nesbit 2012 (57 studies). | Every caption is one short sentence with the number in gold. |
| **Sound in sync with a visual event makes the event stand out.** | Moderate–strong: Van der Burg et al. 2008 ("pip and pop"). The benefit disappears for unrelated sounds. | Effects only on meaningful events: the number landing, real clicks, the counter rolling, the reveal, the cost tone as profit melts. Nothing on cursor moves. |
| **One peak, and a strong ending.** Peak and end moments predict how an experience is remembered; duration barely matters. | Strong: Kahneman et al. 1993; meta-analysis Alaybek et al. 2022 (r ≈ 0.58). | Peak: "Too good to be true?" then the honest twist (January 2026 made the money). End: a true open question that only the live site answers. |
| **Two-sided messages persuade experts, if the objection is answered.** | Moderate (meta-analytic). Raising a doubt without answering it does worse. | The video raises "too good to be true?" and answers it with the crash split, the overfitting check and "we say so". |
| **Invite, don't command.** Forceful calls to action cause reactance. | Moderate: Ma, Ebesu & Dillard (meta-analysis of 35 studies). | "See today's answer:" plus the address and a QR code. No countdowns, no "you must". |
| **Leave a felt gap at the end.** The urge to resume something unfinished. | The Ovsiankina effect is supported; the Zeigarnik memory effect is not (Ghibellini & Meier 2025 meta-analysis), so we do not rely on it. | "Tomorrow's answer isn't in this video. This video stops at 6 Oct 2026. The site doesn't." True, because the site updates every trading day. |
| **Short beats long.** | Moderate: duration neglect (above); hackathon videos are typically 2–3 min. | About 1:45. |

**Sound levels** (mixing practice, not research): music bed well under the captions' moments, effects
14–25 dB below the loudest moment, one loudest effect (the twist), near-silence while the viewer guesses,
a riser that cuts dead on the reveal, a low room tone so pauses don't sound like a fault, and a resolving
chord on the address. The final mix is normalised to −16 LUFS integrated, −1.5 dBTP true peak.

**v3.2, softer sound.** Viewers found the bed sharp. The hi-hats and claps (bright noise bursts) are gone, the
arpeggio and top line are an octave lower and pure tones, the music is rolled off above about 3 kHz and the
effects above 4.5 kHz, ticks and clicks are lower pitched, and the whole mix is 2 dB quieter. Energy above
5 kHz is now 42 dB below the total.

**Visual attention layer (v3.1).** Small, continuous, meaningful motion, so the eye has a reason to stay:
the camera "breathes" (v3.2: about 3% zoom and up to 10 px of drift on 8–13 s cycles), so the picture is never
frozen; edges darken slightly when the camera is close in on a number (a spotlight); a single warm light
sweep crosses each caption as it appears (v3.2: brighter and wider); a brief brightness bloom lands exactly on the four key sounds (the
number, the reveal, the crash, the twist), the same audio-visual synchrony as above; slow, soft gold dust drifts on
the statement cards (v3.2: about 130 fainter specks) and, sparser still, over the site. Each is kept below the level where it reads as an effect rather than as life in the
picture. Sound effects sit under the music bed (v3.1: 8–10 dB quieter than v3), with the twist still the
loudest single sound.

**Avoided:** logo or team intros before the hook; captions that repeat a narration word for word; a sound on
every click; fake urgency; inflated or unexplained numbers; "in conclusion"; credits after the call to action.

Adesope & Nesbit 2012, *Journal of Educational Psychology*; Van der Burg et al. 2008, *JEP: HPP*;
Kahneman et al. 1993, *Psychological Science*; Alaybek et al. 2022, *OBHDP*; Ma, Ebesu & Dillard (reactance
